The **external problem** is simple math: a lower score means higher interest rates, bigger deposits, and more doors closed. On a mortgage, the gap between a fair score and a strong one can cost you tens of thousands of dollars over the life of the loan. The **internal problem** is heavier. It's the shame. The feeling that you blew it, that the damage is permanent, that you'll be paying for old mistakes forever. That feeling keeps people from even logging in to look.

## Know What Actually Drives the Number

A FICO score comes from five things. **Payment history** (paying on time) is about 35%. **Amounts owed**, mostly your credit utilization, is about 30%. Length of credit history is about 15%. New credit and your mix of account types are about 10% each.

Read that again. Two factors make up roughly two-thirds of your score: paying on time and not maxing out your cards. That's where your effort goes. Everything else is fine-tuning.

<div class="pull-quote">Your credit score isn't a verdict on who you are. It's a report card on a few habits, and habits can change.</div>

## Step 1: Check Your Credit Reports for Errors

You can't fix what you haven't looked at. Pull your reports from all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion) for free at AnnualCreditReport.com. That's the official site, and you can check as often as weekly.

Go line by line. Look for accounts you don't recognize, late payments you know you made on time, or wrong balances. If you find one, dispute it directly with the bureau online. A removed error is one of the fastest legitimate ways to **raise your credit score fast**, and it costs you nothing but an afternoon.

## Step 2: Never Miss Another Payment

Payment history is the heavyweight. One payment that's 30 or more days late can knock a good score down hard, and it can stay on your report for up to seven years.

So make on-time payments automatic. Set every card and loan to autopay at least the minimum. Then pay more by hand when you can. The autopay is your safety net, not your plan. If you've already missed a payment, get current right away. And if it's one slip on an otherwise clean record, call the lender and ask for a goodwill adjustment. Asking costs you one phone call.

## Step 3: Cut Your Credit Utilization

**Credit utilization** is the share of your available credit you're using. A $3,000 balance on a $10,000 limit is 30%. Keep it under 30% at a minimum. Under 10% is where the best scores live.

This is the fastest lever you have, because utilization has no memory. Pay the balances down and your score can respond within a month or two. Two moves make it work:

- **Pay before the statement closes.** Your card company usually reports the balance on your statement date, not your due date. Pay it down a few days before the statement closes and a lower number gets reported.
- **Attack the highest-interest card first.** Every dollar off that balance lowers utilization and saves you real interest at the same time.

## Step 4: Ask for a Credit Limit Increase (and Keep Old Cards Open)

If you've had a card for six months or more with a clean payment record, call and ask for a **credit limit increase**. A higher limit with the same balance means lower utilization, and you did nothing but make a phone call. Ask whether they'll do it with a soft pull so it doesn't create a hard inquiry.

One warning: this only works if you don't spend into the new room. A bigger limit is a tool, not a raise.

While you're at it, stop closing old cards. Closing your oldest account can shorten your credit history and shrink your available credit, which raises utilization. If a card has no annual fee, keep it open, put one small recurring bill on it, and set it to autopay.

## Step 5: Stop Applying for New Credit and Let Time Work

Every application for a card or loan can trigger a hard inquiry. One won't wreck you, but a string of them in a short window tells lenders you're scrambling. While you rebuild, hold off on new credit unless you truly need it.

So **how long does it take to rebuild credit**? Utilization changes can show up in 30 to 45 days. A clean run of on-time payments starts to matter within six months to a year. Big negatives fade in weight as they age, long before they drop off the report. The people who win this aren't the ones who find a shortcut. They're the ones who stay steady long enough for the math to catch up.

## What's a Good Credit Score for a Mortgage?

Most conventional loans want a score in the 620s at minimum, and FHA loans can go lower. But the minimum just gets you in the door. The best rates usually go to borrowers in the mid-700s and up. If buying a home is on your horizon, a few months of focused work on your score before you apply can be worth more than any rate shopping you do after.
