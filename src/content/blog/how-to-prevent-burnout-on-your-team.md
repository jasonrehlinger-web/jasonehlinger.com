<p class="lede">Your best person used to be the first one to volunteer. Now they're quiet in meetings, short in their emails, and you caught them staring at the wall at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday. <strong>That's not a bad attitude. That's burnout.</strong> Here's how to prevent burnout on your team before it costs you the people you can least afford to lose.</p>

If you're reading this, you've probably already felt it. The energy on your team is lower than it should be. Deadlines are slipping. People who used to push back with good ideas just nod and go back to their desks.

The external problem is easy to see: work is getting done slower and with more mistakes. The internal problem is harder to say out loud. You're worried you caused it. Maybe you've been leaning hardest on the people who never say no, and part of you knows it. And if you're honest, you might be running on fumes yourself.

I've been on both sides of this. In the military, I watched good soldiers run themselves into the ground because nobody stopped to ask how they were holding up. Then I built and ran a seven-figure company and made the same mistake with my own people. Burnout isn't a personal weakness. It's a leadership problem, which means it's a problem you can actually fix.

## The signs of employee burnout most managers miss

Burnout rarely announces itself. Nobody walks into your office and says, "I'm burned out." Instead you get quieter signals:

**The overachiever goes quiet.** Your top performer stops raising their hand. They still deliver, but just barely, and the spark is gone.

**Small mistakes stack up.** Typos, missed handoffs, forgotten follow-ups. One is a bad day. A pattern is a warning.

**Cynicism creeps in.** "Why bother? Nothing's going to change anyway." When you hear that, pay attention.

**Sick days and late starts climb.** People who are running empty start pulling back physically before they pull back for good.

If you're seeing two or more of these in the same person, don't wait for a performance review. Talk to them this week.

## What actually causes burnout at work

Gallup's research points to a short list of root causes, and almost all of them trace back to how people are managed: unfair treatment, an unmanageable workload, unclear communication, lack of support from the manager, and unreasonable time pressure.

Notice what's not on that list: hard work. People can work hard for a long time when the work is clear, the load is fair, and they know their leader has their back. What breaks them is working hard inside a system that keeps piling on with no end in sight.

<div class="pull-quote">People don't burn out from hard work. They burn out from hard work that never ends and never gets noticed.</div>

## The 5-step plan to prevent burnout on your team

You don't need a big HR program. You need a few habits you run every week. This is the plan I coach leaders through.

### 1. Listen first, and make it a habit

Use your one-on-ones to ask one simple question: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how sustainable is your workload right now?" Then shut up and listen. Don't fix, don't defend. Just understand. When people know they can tell you the truth without getting labeled as weak, they'll tell you before they hit the wall instead of after.

### 2. Audit the workload, not the effort

Write down everything each person is carrying. All of it, including the "small" side projects you handed them in the hallway. Most managers are shocked by what they see. Managing team workload starts with seeing it clearly. If one person is carrying twice what everyone else is, that's not dedication. That's a design flaw.

### 3. Cut, delegate, or delay something

You can't add hours to the day, so something has to give. For every overloaded person, pick one thing to stop, one thing to hand off, and one thing to push to next quarter. Your best people will rarely ask for this. You have to offer it.

### 4. Make priorities painfully clear

Unclear priorities are a quiet burnout machine. When everything is urgent, people try to do everything, and they fail at all of it. Tell your team the top three things that matter this month. If something new comes in, say out loud what it replaces.

### 5. Recognize the work, and protect the recovery

Say thank you, specifically and often. "Great job" is fine. "The way you handled that client yesterday saved us the account" is better. Then protect time off. If you're sending emails at 10 p.m., your team thinks they should be answering them. Model the pace you want them to keep.

## Burnout and employee retention go hand in hand

Here's the hard truth. Your burned-out people aren't going to complain forever. At some point they'll stop complaining and start interviewing. And the ones who leave first are usually the ones with the most options, which means your strongest performers.

When you work on how to reduce employee burnout, you're also working on retention, morale, and quality all at once. It's one of the highest-return things a leader can do. ## What's at stake

Let burnout run and you know how this ends. Your best people leave. The ones who stay check out. Mistakes reach your customers, and you end up doing three jobs yourself, which is how leaders burn out too.

Now picture the other side. Your people tell you early when they're stretched, because they trust you'll do something about it. Workloads are fair. Priorities are clear. People take real time off and come back sharp. Your team does hard things, recovers, and does them again. That's a team that stays together, and a team you'll be proud to lead.

You don't have to fix everything this week. Start with one conversation and one question. Then run the plan.
