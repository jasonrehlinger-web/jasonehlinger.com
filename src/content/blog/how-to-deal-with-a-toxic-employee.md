<p class="lede">You know exactly who I'm talking about. The one who makes every meeting a little tenser, every win a little smaller, every new hire a little more nervous. You've been telling yourself it's a personality thing, or a rough patch, or <em>"that's just how they are."</em> Meanwhile your best people are getting quieter. That's not a coincidence.</p>

Figuring out how to deal with a toxic employee is one of the hardest tests a leader faces, because the problem rarely announces itself. It leaks. It shows up as eye rolls in meetings, side conversations after decisions, sarcasm dressed up as honesty, and a team that slowly stops bringing you problems.

The external problem is obvious: one person's behavior is dragging down the group. The internal problem is the one you don't say out loud. You're afraid of the confrontation. You're afraid you'll handle it badly, or that they'll quit and leave you short-handed, or that HR will make it your fault. So you wait. And every week you wait, your team learns something you never meant to teach them: that the standard doesn't apply to everyone.

I've had to handle this in uniform and in business. Twenty years in the military and years running a seven-figure company taught me that avoiding this conversation never once made it easier. There is a way through that doesn't require you to become a hardcase or a pushover. It requires a plan.

## Signs of a toxic employee (and what they're not)

Be careful here, because not every difficult employee is a toxic one. Someone who disagrees with you in a meeting isn't toxic. Someone who's struggling with performance isn't toxic. Someone going through a divorce who's short-tempered for a month isn't toxic. Those are coaching situations, and they deserve patience.

Toxic behavior in the workplace is a pattern, not a moment. Watch for these:

**They punish honesty.** People stop speaking up around them because the cost is too high. Sarcasm, gossip, and retaliation do the enforcement.

**They divide the room.** Cliques form. There's the group that's "in" with them and the group that isn't. Information becomes currency.

**They're different in front of you.** You hear about the behavior secondhand because it only happens when you're not watching. That gap between what you see and what your team experiences is the tell.

**They make accountability personal.** Every piece of feedback becomes an attack on them, a flaw in the process, or someone else's fault. Nothing ever lands.

The hardest version is the high performer with a bad attitude. They hit their numbers, so you look away. But your team is doing the math you're avoiding: this person's results matter more to you than how they're treated. That math is why your best people leave.

<div class="pull-quote">Whatever behavior you tolerate from your top performer becomes the real standard. Everything else is just a poster on the wall.</div>

## The five-step plan to deal with a toxic employee

You don't need to be a lawyer or a therapist. You need to be clear, consistent, and documented. Here's the path I coach leaders through.

### 1. Get specific before you get vocal

"Bad attitude" is not actionable. Write down the behaviors: what was said, what was done, when, and who it affected. Three to five concrete examples. If you can't name specific behavior, you're not ready for the conversation, and a vague accusation will put them on defense and you on your heels.

### 2. Have the direct conversation, in private, soon

Not an ambush and not a hint. Sit down one-on-one and name the pattern plainly: "In Tuesday's meeting you dismissed Sarah's idea before she finished. That's the third time this month. Here's the impact it's having." Then stop talking and listen. Sometimes there's a real issue underneath, a health problem, a conflict you didn't know about, a sense they've been passed over. You're not excusing the behavior. You're finding out what you're actually dealing with.

### 3. Set the standard and the consequence

Be explicit about what changes, by when, and what happens if it doesn't. "I need meetings where people can disagree with you safely. If this pattern continues, the next conversation is a formal one." Most leaders skip the consequence because it feels harsh. Skipping it is why nothing changes.

### 4. Document employee behavior as you go

After every incident and every conversation, write a short dated note: what happened, what was discussed, what was agreed. This isn't about building a case. It's about fairness and memory. If things improve, the notes die quietly. If they don't, you'll be glad you weren't relying on recollection.

### 5. Decide on a deadline, not a feeling

Give the change 30 to 60 days with honest check-ins. If the behavior turns around, say so and mean it. People can change, and a leader who catches someone doing it right rebuilds loyalty fast. But if nothing moves, you already know when to let an employee go: when keeping them costs you the people who are doing it right. Make the call. Your team is watching either way.

## What's at stake if you wait

Here's what failure looks like: your strongest people leave first, because they have options. The ones who stay learn to keep their heads down. You end up managing a quieter, smaller, more cynical version of the team you built, and the toxic employee is still there, now with proof that nothing happens.

Here's what success looks like: either the behavior changes and you've reclaimed a capable team member, or the person leaves and the room exhales. Leaders tell me the same thing after it's done: "I should have acted six months earlier." The meetings get lighter. Ideas come back. Your best people notice that the standard is real, and they stay.

You're not a bad leader because you have a toxic employee. You become one by keeping them without a plan.
