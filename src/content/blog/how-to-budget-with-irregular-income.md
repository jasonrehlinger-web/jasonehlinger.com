<p class="lede">A great month rolls in and you feel rich. Six weeks later a client pays late, the commission check comes in light, and you're moving money around to cover the mortgage. Same job. Same effort. Completely different feeling. If you've ever wondered why you can't get traction even in a good year, it's not discipline — it's that you're trying to run a <em>fixed</em> life on a <em>variable</em> paycheck with a budget built for someone who gets paid the same amount every other Friday.</p>

Most budgeting advice assumes a salary. Take your monthly income, subtract expenses, done. That advice is useless to a contractor, a commissioned salesperson, a freelancer, a small business owner, or anyone whose pay swings 40% from one month to the next.

So people with variable income do the natural thing: they budget around their *average* month. And that's the mistake. An average is a number that almost never actually shows up in your bank account. Half your months land below it — and those are the months that put things on the credit card.

Here's the good news. Irregular income isn't a curse. It's just a different problem, and it has a clean solution: stop budgeting off what you *might* make and start budgeting off what you can *count on*.

## The Real Problem Isn't the Income — It's the Timing

Your **external problem** is obvious: money arrives unevenly and bills don't. Rent doesn't care that the invoice is 45 days out.

But the **internal problem** is the one that actually costs you sleep. It's the low-grade financial anxiety of never knowing whether this is a good year or a bad one until it's over. It's making a decision — hire someone, take the vacation, buy the house — with a knot in your stomach because you can't tell if the last three months were the trend or the exception.

I've lived on both sides of this. Military pay was the most predictable money I'll ever earn. Building a seven-figure company was the least. What I learned running the second one is that variable income doesn't require more willpower. It requires a buffer between when money arrives and when you spend it.

<div class="pull-quote">You don't need a steady income. You need a steady paycheck — and you can build that yourself.</div>

## The Plan: How to Budget With Irregular Income in 5 Steps

This is the exact system I walk clients through. It takes an evening to set up and then quietly runs in the background.

### 1. Find your baseline — your lowest reliable month

Pull the last 12 months of deposits. Not your best month. Not your average. Find the **lowest month you'd honestly expect to repeat** — usually somewhere near your second- or third-worst month, tossing out any true fluke.

If your worst month was $4,200 and your average is $6,000, your baseline is $4,200. That's the number your budget gets built on. It will feel painfully conservative. That's the point. A budget that survives your worst month survives every month.

### 2. Get your fixed costs under the baseline

Add up everything that must be paid regardless of how the month goes: housing, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, groceries, transportation. That total has to fit inside your baseline. If it doesn't, you don't have a budgeting problem — you have a cost structure problem, and no spreadsheet will fix it. Cut, renegotiate, or raise income until the math works.

### 3. Separate the accounts — income in one place, life in another

This is the move that changes everything. All income lands in a **holding account**. Nothing gets spent from there. Ever.

From that account you immediately split every deposit: taxes off the top, then your fixed monthly transfer to checking. What's left just sits and accumulates. Big months don't feel like a windfall anymore, because you never see them in your spending account.

### 4. Pay yourself a real salary

On the same day each month, transfer your baseline amount from the holding account into checking. That's your paycheck. Now you budget like a salaried person, because functionally you are one — you just happen to be your own payroll department.

Early on you may not have enough in the holding account to smooth out a bad month. That's fine. Build toward one month of buffer, then two, then three. Once you're a full month ahead, you're spending money you already earned, and the whole thing stops being scary.

### 5. Give the surplus a job before it shows up

Good months will pile up cash in the holding account. Decide *now* where it goes, because "I'll figure it out" always turns into "where did it go?" A simple order that works for most people: fill the tax account, top the buffer to your target, then split the rest between debt payoff, sinking funds, and investing.

## Two Things Variable Earners Get Wrong

**Taxes.** If you're 1099, commissioned, or self-employed, nobody is withholding for you. Set aside **25–30% of every single deposit** the moment it lands — more if your state takes a bite. Not quarterly. Not at year-end. Every deposit, into a separate account you pretend doesn't exist. The fastest way to wreck a good year is to spend the government's share of it.

**Emergency fund size.** The standard advice is three to six months of expenses. If your income is variable, that's not enough. Aim for **six to twelve months**, because your risk isn't just losing a job — it's a slow quarter, a client that vanishes, a season that doesn't come. And keep that fund separate from your income buffer. The buffer smooths normal swings; the emergency fund is for actual trouble.

## What's Actually at Stake

Without a system, irregular income runs you. You over-spend in the good months because it feels like the new normal, then scramble in the lean ones. You never build anything, because every surplus gets eaten by the next shortfall. And you carry that knot in your stomach into every business and family decision you make.

With a baseline, a holding account, and a paycheck you write yourself, the swings stop reaching your life. A slow month becomes a data point, not a crisis. A great month becomes progress you can actually see — debt gone, buffer full, money invested — instead of a blur you can't account for.

You already do the hard part: you go out and create the income. This is just building a container that holds it.
