## How Does a Roth IRA Work vs. a Traditional IRA?

Both are individual retirement accounts. Both let your investments grow without a tax bill every year. Both share the same **IRA contribution limits for 2026**: $7,500, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older. The difference is when the IRS gets paid.

**Traditional IRA: pay the tax later.** You may get a **traditional IRA tax deduction** now, which lowers this year's tax bill. The money grows tax-deferred. When you pull it out in retirement, every dollar is taxed as ordinary income. And starting at age 73, the government requires you to take money out whether you need it or not.

**Roth IRA: pay the tax now.** You contribute money you've already paid tax on. No deduction today. But the money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement (after age 59½, with the account open at least five years) are completely tax-free. No required withdrawals during your lifetime either.

Think of it like a farmer. Do you want to pay tax on the seed, or on the harvest? With a Roth, you pay on the seed and the whole harvest is yours.

<div class="pull-quote">The best IRA is the one you actually fund. Picking the "wrong" one costs far less than picking none.</div>

## Roth IRA Income Limits 2026: Can You Even Contribute?

Before you weigh pros and cons, check eligibility. The Roth has income limits. For 2026, your ability to contribute phases out between:

- **$153,000 and $168,000** of modified adjusted gross income if you're single or head of household
- **$242,000 and $252,000** if you're married filing jointly

Earn above the top of that range and you can't contribute to a Roth directly. A traditional IRA has no income limit to contribute, but if you or your spouse have a retirement plan at work, your ability to *deduct* it phases out at much lower incomes (for 2026, between $81,000 and $91,000 for a single filer covered at work).

That catches a lot of people. If you can't deduct a traditional IRA contribution, you get the tax bill later with none of the break now. In that case the Roth is usually the clear winner if you qualify.

## Which Is Better, Roth or Traditional IRA? Your 4-Step Plan

### 1. Compare Your Tax Rate Now vs. Later

This is the heart of it. If you expect to be in a **lower** tax bracket in retirement than you are today, the traditional IRA usually wins. Take the deduction now while your rate is high. If you expect to be in the **same or higher** bracket later, the Roth usually wins.

Early in your career, or in a lower-income year, your rate is probably as low as it will ever be. That's prime Roth territory. When I was making a military salary, the Roth was an easy call. Paying tax at a low rate now and never again later is a great trade.

### 2. Check What You Already Have

Look at your workplace plan. If most of your retirement money is already in a pre-tax 401(k) or TSP, adding a Roth gives you **tax diversification**. In retirement you'll be able to pull from a taxable bucket and a tax-free bucket, depending on what keeps your tax bill lowest that year. That flexibility is worth a lot.

### 3. Think About Access

Here's a detail that matters for real families. With a Roth, you can withdraw your **contributions** (not the earnings) at any time without tax or penalty. It isn't an emergency fund and shouldn't be treated like one. But knowing that money isn't locked away until 59½ makes a lot of people more willing to start. Traditional IRA withdrawals before 59½ generally get taxed and hit with a 10% penalty.

### 4. Pick One and Automate It

If you're still torn after steps 1 through 3, here's my default for most people I coach: **if you qualify, start with the Roth.** Then set up an automatic monthly contribution. $625 a month maxes out a 2026 IRA. $200 a month is a strong start. Automation beats perfect timing every time.

## The Mistake That Costs More Than the Wrong Account

Here's what keeps me up at night for the people I work with. It's not that someone picks a Roth when a traditional would have saved them a little. It's the person who spends three years "researching" and never opens either one.

Run the math. $500 a month for 30 years at a 7% average return grows to roughly $600,000. Wait five years to start and you end up with closer to $400,000. That five years of hesitation costs around $200,000. No tax strategy on earth makes up for that.

So **should you open a Roth IRA** or a traditional? Answer the four questions above, make the call, and fund it this month. You can always adjust next year. You can't get back the years you didn't invest.
