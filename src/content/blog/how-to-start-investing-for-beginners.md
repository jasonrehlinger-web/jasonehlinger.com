<p class="lede">You've read the articles. You know you're "supposed to" invest. That's not a discipline problem. That's an information overload problem, and it's the most expensive kind.

The external problem is simple: you don't know where to start investing. The internal problem is the one that actually keeps you stuck — you're afraid of looking stupid, afraid of losing money, afraid that everyone else got a manual you never received. So you wait. And waiting, it turns out, is the single most costly investing decision you can make.

I spent twenty years in the military and didn't touch a real investment account until embarrassingly late. Then I built a seven-figure company and spent years coaching people through this exact wall. Here's what I know now that I wish someone had told me at 25: **you do not need to be smart to invest. You need to be consistent.** The market rewards the person who starts and stays in, not the person who waited until they understood everything.

## How much money do you need to start investing?

Less than you think. This is the myth that keeps most people on the sidelines — the belief that investing is for people with a spare $10,000 and a guy named Chad who manages their portfolio.

It isn't. Most major brokerages now let you open an account with nothing and buy fractional shares for a few dollars. If investing 15% of your income feels impossible right now, that's fine. Start with $25 a month. Start with $50. The dollar amount barely matters at the beginning — **the habit is the asset you're actually building.** The number goes up later, on its own, once starting stops feeling scary.

## The five-step plan to start investing this week

You don't need a finance degree. You need the same five moves, in order. Do them once and the hard part is behind you forever.

### 1. Cover your foundation first

Before you invest a dollar, make sure you're not building on sand. That means a small emergency fund — even $1,000 to start — and no high-interest credit card debt eating you alive at 24%. Paying off a 24% card is a guaranteed 24% return; no investment reliably beats that. If that's your situation, kill the card first, then come back here.

### 2. Use the free money before the clever money

If your employer offers a 401(k) match, that's your first stop, full stop. A 100% match is the only place on earth you double your money the instant you invest it. Contribute at least enough to get the full match. Skipping it is turning down a raise.

### 3. Keep it boring: buy the whole market

Here's the part that makes people nervous, and it shouldn't. You are not going to pick the next hot stock. Neither am I, and neither is most of Wall Street. Instead, buy a **low-cost index fund** — a single fund that owns a tiny slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once. When you buy the whole market, you stop betting on one horse and start owning the whole track. Boring is the strategy. Boring wins.

### 4. Automate it so you never have to be brave again

Willpower is a terrible investment plan. Set up an automatic transfer the day after payday — before you can spend it, before you can talk yourself out of it. This is **dollar-cost averaging**: you buy a fixed amount on a schedule, in good months and scary ones. It removes the one thing that ruins beginners — the urge to time the market. You will never buy at the perfect moment. Automation means you never have to try.

### 5. Then leave it alone

The hardest step is the one where you do nothing. You'll be tempted to check it daily, to sell when the news is ugly, to tinker. Don't. The people who win aren't the ones who react fastest — they're the ones who set it up right and then get out of their own way for a decade.

## What the fear is really about

Let's name the thing under the surface. Most people who "can't figure out investing" aren't confused about index funds. They're carrying a quiet belief that they're bad with money — that they'll screw this up like they think they've screwed up other financial decisions. So not starting feels safer than starting and failing.

I've coached enough people to tell you plainly: that story is wrong, and it's costing you the most valuable asset you have, which is time. You're not bad with money. You were never taught this, and nobody hands you a manual. That's a gap in your education, not a flaw in your character — and gaps close fast once you take the first step.

## What starting changes

Here's the stakes, honest version. Stay on the sidelines and nothing bad happens today — that's exactly why it's dangerous. You feel it in fifteen years, when the money that could have quietly grown into a down payment or an early retirement is just... not there. Waiting doesn't announce its cost. It just collects it.

Start, and something shifts almost immediately. Not in your balance — that grows slowly at first — but in you. You stop feeling like money is a test you're failing. You watch your first automatic transfer go through and realize the scary thing was never that hard. You become the kind of person who has an investing habit, and that identity compounds even faster than the money does.

Fifteen minutes. One boring index fund. One automatic transfer. That's the whole game at the start.

You don't need to understand everything. You just need to begin.
