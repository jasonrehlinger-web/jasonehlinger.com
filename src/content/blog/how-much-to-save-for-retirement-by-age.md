## The one rule that matters most

Before the age-by-age benchmarks, understand the rule they're all built on: **save about 15% of your gross income every year, including any employer match.**

That's the number Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, and most credible sources land on, and it assumes you start around 25. Start later and the percentage climbs. But 15% is the anchor. If your employer matches 4% and you put in 11%, you're there. If there's no match, 15% is on you.

Most people never do the math because they're afraid of the answer. But 15% of a $70,000 salary is about $1,050 a month — and a big chunk of that comes back to you in tax savings and free employer money. It is almost always more doable than the fear suggests.

## Retirement savings benchmarks by age

Here's the map the big investment firms use. These are multiples of your annual salary, saved across all your retirement accounts.

### By 30 — 1x your salary

One year of income saved. If you make $60,000, aim for roughly $60,000 banked. Miss it by a little and you're fine — this is the decade compounding does the most quiet work, so even small contributions matter enormously.

### By 40 — 3x your salary

This is the checkpoint that catches most people off guard. The average 35-to-44-year-old has around $141,000 saved, but the benchmark says three times your income. Behind here? You still have 25-plus years of growth ahead of you. That's a long runway.

### By 50 — 6x your salary

Now you're in your peak earning years, and the IRS lets you make catch-up contributions on top of the normal limits. This is where a disciplined decade can close a shocking amount of ground.

### By 60 — 8x your salary

The finish line is in sight. This is about protecting what you've built and getting precise about the gap, not swinging for the fences.

### By 67 — 10x your salary

Ten times your pre-retirement income is the target most planners use to keep your current lifestyle intact. Hit it and you've bought yourself something most people never get: choices.

## What to do if you're behind

Most people reading this are behind at least one benchmark. Good — now you know. Here's the plan.

**1. Capture every dollar of free money first.** If your employer matches, contribute at least enough to get the full match before anything else. Walking past a match is walking past a raise.

**2. Automate the 15% and forget it.** Willpower fails; automation doesn't. Set the contribution to come out before the money ever hits your checking account. You can't miss what you never see.

**3. Use catch-up contributions after 50.** The tax code specifically rewards late starters. If your 20s and 30s got away from you, your 50s can do more heavy lifting than you'd believe.

**4. Raise your rate every time you get a raise.** Bump your contribution by 1–2% each year or each promotion. You never feel the pinch, and the compounding is brutal in the best way.

**5. Get honest about the lifestyle, not just the number.** Sometimes the fix isn't earning more — it's plugging the leaks that are quietly draining the money that should be building your future.

## What's really at stake

Let's be clear about the cost of doing nothing. Ignore this for another five years and you don't just lose the money you didn't save — you lose the growth that money would have earned, which is the part that actually funds retirement. Compounding rewards the people who start, and it's merciless to the people who wait.

But here's the other side. Get your rate right and stay consistent, and retirement stops being a source of dread and becomes a decision you get to make on your terms — when to stop, how to spend your time, who to help. That's not about being rich. That's about being free.

You don't have to figure this out alone, and you don't have to have it all figured out today. You just have to take the next honest step.
